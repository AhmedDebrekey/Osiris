# Psychological Horror Game — Story Treatment

## Working Concept

A grounded psychological horror game about a struggling man whose life has slowly drifted away from the person he once wanted to become.

As a child, he dreamed of becoming a doctor because he wanted to help people. As an adult, he is struggling with alcohol, drugs, unstable employment, shame, and the consequences of years of bad decisions. He is not portrayed as purely evil or hopeless. There are still traces of the person he once was, but he has spent years suppressing guilt, avoiding responsibility, and telling himself that he will change later.

Now, that guilt is beginning to manifest around him.

He hears voices.

He hears fragments of childhood memories.

He sees people who are not really there.

Some seem to accuse him.

Others seem to warn him.

As the story progresses, the line between hallucination, memory, guilt, and something possibly supernatural becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish.

The game should remain ambiguous. It should never fully confirm whether the figures are supernatural beings, hallucinations caused by addiction and psychological deterioration, manifestations of guilt, or some combination of all three.

The tone should be grounded and slow-burning, similar in spirit to games like *Fears to Fathom*: ordinary places, ordinary routines, and realistic interactions that gradually become disturbing.

The horror comes from noticing that something is wrong before the game tells the player what it is.

---

# Central Themes

The main themes are:

**Guilt.**  
The protagonist knows that he has become someone his younger self would barely recognize.

**Avoidance.**  
He repeatedly escapes from painful emotions rather than confronting them.

**Addiction.**  
Substances initially provide relief but gradually become another force controlling his life.

**Lost potential.**  
The contrast between the innocent child who wanted to help people and the adult he eventually becomes should remain present throughout the game.

**Responsibility.**  
The story should avoid reducing everything to “he is a bad person.” Instead, it should explore the complicated difference between circumstances that hurt him and choices he made himself.

**Self-deception.**  
The protagonist may remember certain events in ways that make himself less responsible. Some memories could later be revisited and shown differently.

**What controls a person.**  
The final question, “Who is your Lord?”, ties into the idea of what a person truly serves through their actions: God, addiction, money, pleasure, fear, pride, approval, or themselves.

---

# Setting

The game takes place in an ordinary modern town or small city.

Nothing about the world should initially feel gothic or traditionally horrific.

The environments should include places such as:

- The protagonist’s small apartment.
- Residential streets.
- Convenience stores.
- His workplace.
- Bus stops or parking areas.
- Friends’ homes.
- A party house.
- Possibly a hospital, clinic, school, or childhood home later in the story.
- Streets at night.
- Locations reconstructed entirely through memory.

The world should feel lived-in but slightly lonely.

Streetlights hum.

Cars pass.

Neighbors can be heard through walls.

Phones vibrate.

Refrigerators buzz.

Televisions play in distant apartments.

The normality of these environments should make the hallucinations feel more invasive.

---

# The Protagonist

The protagonist is an adult man whose exact age is still undecided, likely somewhere in his twenties or thirties.

As a child, he was optimistic.

One recurring memory is:

> “Mom, when I grow up I want to be a doctor.”

His mother asks why.

> “Because I want to help people.”

That sentence represents the person he believed he would become.

The adult protagonist is almost the opposite.

He struggles to stay employed.

He arrives late.

He drinks heavily.

He uses drugs.

He disappoints people.

He avoids calls.

He may owe money.

He may have damaged relationships.

He might still own objects from the life he once imagined: an old biology textbook, childhood drawings, school certificates, or something connected to medicine.

Importantly, he should still occasionally behave kindly.

The player needs to see that the younger version of him has not completely disappeared.

His tragedy is not that he never had goodness in him.

It is that he repeatedly chooses things that bury it.

---

# The Figures

Two broad types of figures currently exist in the concept.

Their exact meaning should remain unclear.

## Dark Figures

The dark figures appear throughout the protagonist’s present-day life.

They resemble people, but something about them immediately feels wrong.

They may stand unnaturally still.

Their faces may be difficult to focus on.

They may stare directly at the protagonist even when everyone around them is behaving normally.

Whenever one appears, the protagonist physically reacts.

His breathing becomes heavier.

His heartbeat grows louder.

The screen may shake subtly.

Ambient sound may become muffled or distorted.

The figures whisper things to him.

Possible phrases include:

> “Look at you.”

> “Late again.”

> “He knows.”

> “You said you’d stop.”

> “Failure.”

> “Nobody believes you.”

> “He hates you.”

At first, they appear to simply accuse him.

Later, however, some may encourage his excuses:

> “It wasn’t your fault.”

> “They deserved it.”

> “Just one drink.”

> “Nobody cares.”

This creates uncertainty about what they represent.

They may be guilt.

They may be temptation.

They may be the lies he tells himself.

They may simply be pieces of his deteriorating mind.

### Their usual behavior

The player sees one standing somewhere ahead.

It locks eyes with him.

It whispers.

The player approaches.

The protagonist’s breathing and heartbeat increase.

The screen becomes unstable.

When the player gets too close—

The figure disappears.

No explanation.

No attack.

No dramatic monster animation.

It simply is no longer there.

The environment immediately becomes normal again.

This becomes an established rule throughout the game.

Eventually, that rule may be broken.

---

# White Figures

The white figures appear primarily in memories.

They should not look like obvious angels.

Instead, they might resemble washed-out human silhouettes, almost like people whose details have been overexposed.

Their voices are faint.

They represent something closer to regret than morality.

Most importantly:

**They speak like the protagonist’s future self looking backward.**

They already know what happens.

They are not simply saying:

> “Don’t do drugs.”

They speak like someone desperately wishing he could reach backward through time and stop himself.

Possible lines:

> “You’ll think about this night for years.”

> “You don’t know what you’re giving up.”

> “You still have time.”

> “Please. Just go home.”

> “This is where you start losing yourself.”

> “You’ll keep telling yourself it was only once.”

> “You won’t remember when it stopped being a choice.”

> “I wish I could stop you.”

> “I wish you could see me.”

One of the last things they might say before his first drug use is:

> “I’m sorry.”

Their voices could subtly resemble an older version of the protagonist’s own voice.

The player may not realize this immediately.

On a second playthrough, it could become obvious.

---

# Opening Sequence

## INT. BEDROOM — MORNING

Darkness.

The sound of breathing.

A faint glow passes through cheap curtains.

The protagonist slowly opens his eyes.

He has overslept.

His room is messy without becoming exaggerated.

Clothes.

Empty bottles.

An alarm he ignored.

Maybe an overdue notice.

Perhaps something from his childhood sits forgotten on a shelf.

Before the player even leaves the bed, voices can be heard from the living room.

A woman.

A child.

The sound is warm.

Domestic.

Almost comforting.

The player initially assumes someone is actually in the apartment.

The child says:

> “Mom, when I grow up I want to be a doctor.”

The mother laughs gently.

> “A doctor?”

> “Yeah.”

> “Why?”

A pause.

> “Because I want to help people.”

The player walks toward the living room.

Perhaps the mother begins another sentence.

The protagonist turns the corner.

The audio stops instantly.

The living room is empty.

No ghost.

No figure.

Nothing supernatural appears.

Just silence.

The protagonist stands there.

Then normal environmental noise slowly returns.

A refrigerator.

Traffic outside.

A neighbor.

The game continues.

---

# The Keys

The protagonist realizes that he is already late for work.

He needs his keys.

This acts as the first small gameplay puzzle.

The player searches the apartment.

The keys are not where they should be.

The player checks several obvious places.

Nothing.

Eventually, after searching the apartment thoroughly, the player returns to somewhere already checked.

The keys are suddenly there.

No animation.

No explanation.

They simply exist now.

The protagonist may mutter something under his breath.

The player begins to understand that the environment cannot always be trusted.

---

# Walking to Work

The protagonist leaves his apartment.

The world outside is completely ordinary.

Cars.

Pedestrians.

Maybe morning traffic.

The player begins walking toward work.

Then, somewhere ahead, a person is standing in the middle of the street.

They are motionless.

They are looking directly at the protagonist.

Everyone else behaves as though nothing is unusual.

The protagonist’s breathing changes.

Heartbeat begins.

The figure whispers.

Possibly:

> “Look at you.”

The player approaches.

The whisper repeats.

> “Look at you.”

Closer.

Heartbeat louder.

Subtle screen shaking.

Then—

The figure disappears.

A vehicle may pass through the exact space where it had been standing.

Street noise returns.

The protagonist keeps walking.

Nothing else happens.

---

# Workplace

For the current version of the story, the protagonist works as a cashier or in another low-paying customer-facing job.

He enters through an employee area.

He clocks in.

He is late again.

While walking toward the counter, his boss stops him.

The boss should not be portrayed as cruel.

The boss is frustrated, but reasonably so.

He may have already given the protagonist several chances.

The conversation begins.

> “You’re late again.”

Behind the boss, another dark figure slowly becomes visible.

The boss continues:

> “This keeps happening.”

The figure whispers:

> “Failure.”

The boss:

> “I’ve covered for you more than once.”

The figure:

> “He hates you.”

The boss:

> “I asked if you needed help.”

The figure:

> “He’s lying.”

The boss:

> “I can’t keep doing this.”

The protagonist now has to concentrate on two voices simultaneously.

The figure may occasionally speak directly over the boss.

It may repeat fragments of what the boss says in a distorted way.

The boss never acknowledges it.

He cannot see it.

Eventually, the protagonist may be fired or given one final warning, depending on how the larger plot develops.

The important point is that the boss is not the villain.

The protagonist has genuinely caused this situation.

---

# The First Drug Memory

At some point later in the game, the protagonist experiences a memory of the first time he used a serious drug.

The player does not necessarily realize immediately that it is a memory.

## INT. WORKPLACE — EVENING — YEARS EARLIER

The protagonist has just finished an exhausting shift.

His phone vibrates.

Messages from friends.

They are having a party.

The conversation implies that he has had a difficult day.

Maybe he originally planned to go home.

His friends tell him to come over.

Eventually, he agrees.

---

# The Party

## INT. HOUSE PARTY — NIGHT

The atmosphere is initially comfortable.

Music.

People talking.

Cheap drinks.

Laughter.

The younger protagonist is still recognizably different from the man we know in the present.

He may be studying.

He may still have ambitions.

He may still talk about his future.

At some point, someone offers him a drug.

This is when the white figures begin appearing.

They are barely visible.

One may stand across the room.

Another might appear in a reflection.

Their voices are so faint that they almost disappear beneath the music.

> “Go home.”

Another:

> “You still have time.”

The player approaches the drug.

Another whisper:

> “You’ll think about this night for years.”

The player can hesitate.

But ultimately the memory cannot progress until he accepts.

Someone may ask:

> “You sure?”

The white figure whispers:

> “Please.”

The protagonist takes it.

The voices begin fading.

One final voice:

> “I’m sorry.”

Silence.

But nothing horrifying happens immediately.

The party continues.

People laugh.

The music keeps playing.

For several minutes, everything seems fine.

---

# First Appearance of the Dark Figure

Someone asks the protagonist to do something irrelevant.

Maybe:

- Find a phone charger.
- Find a bathroom.
- Grab someone’s jacket.
- Get water.
- Find a friend upstairs.

The purpose is simply to make the player explore the house.

He walks away from the party.

The music becomes muffled through walls.

He enters a quieter hallway.

He turns a corner.

A dark figure is standing close to him.

A brief, sharp audio sting.

The protagonist jerks.

Heartbeat explodes.

The figure does not attack.

It simply looks directly at him.

Then it whispers:

> “Now I see you.”

Or another phrase with similar meaning.

Then it disappears.

This is chronologically the first appearance of the dark figures.

The moment suggests that something has changed.

The white voices tried to reach backward.

He ignored them.

Now something else begins following him forward.

---

# Memory as an Unreliable Space

Memories should not necessarily be objective truth.

The player may experience important events more than once.

The first version may reflect how the protagonist prefers to remember them.

The later version may reveal details he suppressed.

For example, during the first version of the party memory, it may appear that his friends heavily pressured him into using.

Later, the player returns to the exact same night.

The room is the same.

The dialogue is different.

Instead of:

> “Come on, everyone does it.”

The friend says:

> “You don’t have to.”

The protagonist replies:

> “No. I want to.”

The implication is not that addiction was entirely his fault.

Instead, the story reveals that he has altered certain memories because accepting his own role is painful.

The horror comes partly from realizing that even the protagonist’s memories cannot be trusted.

---

# Recurring Childhood Memory

The doctor conversation should appear several times.

At first, it is warm.

> “Mom, when I grow up I want to be a doctor.”

> “Why?”

> “Because I want to help people.”

Later, only fragments appear.

> “I want to help people.”

Later still:

> “Help people.”

Eventually, the memory becomes almost confrontational.

The child may ask:

> “Did I become a doctor?”

Or:

> “Do I help people?”

The adult protagonist may be unable to answer.

One particularly painful moment could involve the young version of himself looking directly at the player.

Not at another character.

At the camera.

> “Do I become a doctor?”

Then the memory ends.

---

# General Gameplay Structure

The game should not become a traditional puzzle-heavy horror game.

The core experience should be:

**Exploration.**

**Mundane tasks.**

**Environmental interaction.**

**Dialogue.**

**Phone messages.**

**Memories.**

**Occasional psychological puzzles.**

The horror should invade ordinary life.

Possible tasks include:

Finding keys.

Clocking into work.

Taking out trash.

Buying food.

Responding to text messages.

Walking home.

Searching for an object.

Waiting for transportation.

Cleaning something.

Looking for medication.

Calling someone.

Going to a party.

Finding a bathroom.

The player should often be doing something completely normal when something disturbing occurs.

---

# Puzzle Philosophy

Puzzles should feel psychologically connected to the protagonist.

A useful recurring principle is:

**Every puzzle forces him to reconstruct something he would rather leave incomplete.**

For example, a room from childhood may initially contain objects in the wrong places.

The player must restore them.

When everything is correct, the memory changes.

The protagonist realizes he remembered the event incorrectly.

Another puzzle might involve listening to overlapping voice recordings and arranging them chronologically.

Another could involve searching his apartment for something while objects subtly move whenever the player is not looking.

The goal is not to have complicated locks and arbitrary codes.

The puzzles should reveal character.

---

# Escalation of the Dark Figures

Early game:

One figure.

Far away.

Short whisper.

Disappears quickly.

Mid-game:

Several figures.

Closer.

More personal statements.

Sometimes visible inside buildings.

Sometimes appearing behind real people.

Sometimes reflected in glass.

Later:

They begin appearing where escape is difficult.

They say things only the protagonist could know.

Some start defending him rather than accusing him.

> “It wasn’t your fault.”

> “Forget it.”

> “You deserve another drink.”

This creates the possibility that the figures do not represent one simple force.

Toward the end, they may appear constantly.

The protagonist may struggle to determine which people are real.

A normal person could even trigger the same screen effects.

The player approaches expecting them to disappear.

They do not.

They simply say:

> “…Can I help you?”

---

# The Established Rule

Throughout most of the game, the player learns something important:

**Dark figures disappear when approached.**

They can frighten him.

They can whisper.

They can distort his perception.

But walking directly toward them makes them vanish.

The player becomes accustomed to this.

Eventually, approaching them almost becomes a form of resistance.

This rule becomes extremely important for the ending.

---

# Possible Final Sequence

The exact plot leading here is still under development.

However, the current possible ending is a fatal traffic accident.

By this stage, the protagonist has experienced increasingly intense hallucinations and memories.

He has been forced to confront parts of his life that he spent years suppressing.

Perhaps he has lost his job.

Perhaps relationships have collapsed.

Perhaps he has attempted to stop drinking or using and failed.

Perhaps he has finally begun understanding the pattern of his life.

He walks outside at night.

Traffic passes.

Then he sees one final dark figure.

It is standing across or in the middle of the road.

It looks at him.

The familiar heartbeat begins.

The familiar breathing.

The familiar distortion.

The player knows what to do.

Approach it.

The figure whispers:

> “Look at you.”

The player steps into the road.

The figure does not disappear.

Closer.

Heartbeat becomes overwhelming.

The protagonist says:

> “What do you want from me?”

The figure continues staring.

Perhaps it answers:

> “You still don’t understand.”

For the first time, the player is close enough to touch it.

It remains there.

Then—

Headlights.

A horn.

Impact.

Immediate darkness.

---

# Death

No dramatic cinematic shot is necessary.

The player experiences the aftermath entirely through sound.

Muffled voices.

Someone shouting:

> “Call an ambulance!”

Footsteps.

Confusion.

Sirens in the distance.

The protagonist’s breathing.

The sirens become louder.

Then strangely quieter.

Perhaps a paramedic speaks to him.

The words become increasingly difficult to understand.

The protagonist’s breathing slows.

Heartbeat fades.

Eventually—

Nothing.

Complete silence.

Several seconds pass.

Then a voice speaks.

Unlike the hallucinations, it is completely clear.

No distortion.

No echo.

No panic.

Possibly:

> “Who is your Lord?”

Black screen.

Credits.

---

# Meaning of the Ending

The exact meaning should remain open to interpretation.

The voice could represent God.

Judgment.

His final hallucination.

A childhood religious memory.

His conscience.

Or something supernatural that was present throughout the entire story.

The question does not necessarily only mean:

“Which religion did you follow?”

It can also mean:

**What actually ruled your life?**

What did you obey?

What controlled your decisions?

What did you return to whenever you were afraid?

Addiction?

Pleasure?

Fear?

Shame?

Pride?

Approval?

Yourself?

The game should not necessarily answer the question.

The player should leave thinking about it.

---

# Visual Style

The visual presentation should remain relatively realistic.

The world should not immediately become surreal.

Distortions should be restrained early on.

Examples:

Slight camera trembling.

Subtle chromatic distortion.

Temporary focus problems.

Light flickering.

Environmental sounds becoming muffled.

Faces becoming difficult to read.

Long silhouettes at the edge of the player’s vision.

Objects appearing somewhere after already being checked.

A person disappearing after the camera briefly looks away.

Mirrors showing something slightly different.

As the protagonist deteriorates, these effects can gradually become stronger.

---

# Sound Design

Sound should be one of the most important horror tools in the game.

The player should frequently hear things before seeing anything.

Recurring sounds could include:

Heavy breathing.

Heartbeat.

Whispers.

Childhood conversations.

Distant television.

Traffic.

Party music through walls.

Phones vibrating.

Someone walking in another room.

A mother calling from somewhere that should be empty.

The disappearing figures should not always use jumpscare sounds.

Most encounters should be quiet.

The rare sudden audio sting becomes much more effective if it is not constantly used.

The first dark figure at the party could be one of those rare moments.

---

# Phone

The protagonist’s phone can provide much of the exposition naturally.

Instead of cutscenes explaining relationships, the player can read text messages.

Messages could reveal:

Friends inviting him out.

Missed calls from his mother.

Messages from employers.

Someone asking whether he is okay.

Someone angry that he borrowed money.

Someone saying:

> “You promised.”

Older messages might reveal the gradual deterioration of relationships.

The phone can also become unreliable later.

Messages could appear that the protagonist does not remember receiving.

A conversation might change.

A text from a dead or estranged person could appear.

Whether these are hallucinations remains unclear.

---

# Tone

The game should avoid portraying the protagonist as a cartoonishly miserable addict from the first minute.

There should be humor.

Normal conversations.

Kind people.

Quiet moments.

Maybe beautiful moments.

The player needs to understand what there is to lose.

The horror becomes stronger when it interrupts ordinary life.

The protagonist should sometimes feel almost normal.

Then something happens.

A voice.

A figure.

A memory.

And the player remembers that his mind is becoming increasingly unstable.

---

# Important Story Principle

The dark figures should not be treated like conventional monsters.

They do not chase the player through hallways.

They do not have health bars.

They are not enemies that can be defeated.

They exist to make the player question what they are seeing and what the protagonist is refusing to acknowledge.

Likewise, the story should not simply say:

**Drugs made his life bad.**

The larger tragedy is that he repeatedly used escape as his answer to suffering.

Substances are one part of that pattern.

So are lies.

Avoiding people.

Ignoring consequences.

Blaming others.

Suppressing guilt.

The central question becomes:

**How long can someone run from themselves before there is nowhere left to go?**

---

# Current Story Skeleton

### Act I — Something Is Wrong

The protagonist wakes late.

He hears the childhood conversation with his mother.

The apartment is empty.

He searches for his keys.

They appear somewhere already searched.

He walks to work.

He encounters the first present-day dark figure.

At work, another figure appears behind his boss during a confrontation.

His ordinary life begins falling apart.

### Act II — What Happened to You?

The protagonist’s past begins intruding more aggressively.

The player experiences memories.

The party where he first used drugs is shown.

White figures appear and speak with the regret of his future self.

He ignores them.

The first dark figure appears afterward.

The player begins reconstructing his decline.

Some memories are later revealed to be inaccurate.

Relationships and previous choices become clearer.

The protagonist begins realizing that he has been lying to himself about certain parts of his life.

### Act III — Look at Yourself

Present and memory begin blending together.

Figures appear constantly.

Normal people become difficult to distinguish from hallucinations.

Childhood memories become more direct.

His younger self confronts him.

The protagonist is forced to see the difference between who he wanted to become and who he became.

The dark figures become increasingly impossible to escape.

Eventually he encounters the final figure in the road.

The player follows the behavior they have learned throughout the entire game.

Approach.

This time, the figure does not disappear.

A car hits him.

Sirens.

Darkness.

Silence.

> “Who is your Lord?”

Credits.

---

# Status

This is a working story treatment, not a locked script.

Major elements may change during development.

The most established ideas currently are:

The protagonist wanted to become a doctor as a child.

His adult life has deteriorated through addiction, avoidance, and repeated moral compromise.

Childhood memories haunt him.

Dark human-like figures appear throughout his present life.

They stare, whisper, and disappear when approached.

White figures appear around the memory of his first drug use and speak like his future self regretting what happened.

The protagonist’s memories may be unreliable.

The horror remains ambiguous between psychological and supernatural explanations.

The story gradually forces him to confront the difference between who he wanted to be and who he became.

The current possible ending is a traffic accident caused when he approaches a dark figure standing in the road.

As he dies, everything fades into darkness.

A final voice asks:

> **“Who is your Lord?”**
